18世纪英国小说的兴起

2026春 · FORE50171.01 · Russell Benjamin Palmer

来源：复旦教务系统教学大纲；正文整理。

课程简介（中文）

本课程探究英语小说于十七世纪末至十八世纪初在英格兰的起源。有别于诸多关于小说何以在十八世纪早期“兴起”的争论性理论，本课程致力于展现早期小说形态的多样性。借鉴当前学术研究动向，课程将形式主义的文学考量与印刷文化研究相结合，将小说置于其生产（作者、书商）与消费（读者）的具体历史语境中加以考察。所选经典研读文本皆为其时代小说类型的典范之作。

课程简介（英文）

This course explores the beginnings of the English novel as they occurred in late seventeenth- and early eighteenth-century England. Rather than focusing on the many competing theories about how, why, and whether the novel “rose” in the early eighteenth century, this course endeavors to showcase the varied forms the emergent novel took. Drawing on current research directions, the course balances formal literary considerations with print culture concerns, situating the novel within its contexts of production (authorship, booksellers) and consumption (readers). Each of the well-known and canonical study texts exemplify their kind.

教学目标

The course aims to offer students an up-to-date introduction to the early English novel by prioritizing (1) the formal development of a literary genre, (2) the development of the novel as part of an expanding print culture, and (3) the social and cultural contexts of production and consumption. Starting in the late seventeenth century with Aphra Behn’s Oroonoko and ending with Henry Fielding’s first novel, Joseph Andrews, the course situates the establishment of the novel within the multiple and changing readerships of printed fiction, charting the novel’s development into a legitimate and popular literary genre. In addition to critical and close reading skills, the course also offers students a robust understanding of early eighteenth-century print culture. The primary novels studies will be Aphra Behn’s Oroonoko (1688), Daniel Defoe’s Robinson Crusoe (1719) and Moll Flanders (1722), Samuel Richardson’s Pamela; or, Virtue Rewarded (1740), and Henry Fielding’s Joseph Andrews (1742).

教学方式

The course consists of lectures and seminars. In the latter, students analyze and discuss the course texts based on their reading and in-class examples. Weekly assigned readings (primary and secondary literature) form a crucial part of this course: the readings are supported by online digital quizzes and also support in-class discussion. Theoretical ideas are introduced slowly and formal literary analysis focuses on character, realism and mode. Students will learn to examine literary texts both verbally and visually. Each novel will be examined in multiple sessions, allowing students to become familiar with the text and providing ample time for reading.

教学内容与进度安排

A Changing Literary Form: Restoration Romance. This introductory session acquaints students with the aims, contents, and requirements of the course, before introducing of Aphra Behn’s Oroonoko (1688). The text is positioned to trouble the idea of a simple “rise” from the beginning of the course. What is the literary identity of Oroonoko? Romance, amatory fiction, proto-novel? A Changing World: Colonialism, Capitalism, Race. Behn’s Oroonoko is used to explain the students the radical changes occurring in the period and the English world view, especially in terms of expansionism, profit, and slavery. Fact, Fiction, and Robinson Crusoe. This session relates Robinson Crusoe to the pre-existing literary and non-literary genres on which Defoe drew, before examining how Defoe attempted to create verisimilitude (or circumstantial realism). It also explains how Robinson Crusoe came to be a blueprint form many future novels (robinsonades). The Individual in Fiction. This session continues the discussion of realism in Robinson Crusoe, relating it formally to the relationship between the first-person narrative told in retrospect and realism, individuality and the journal, and readerly identification. Thematically, the session will briefly address the connections between individualism, commercialism, and colonial ambition. The Picaresque Novel. This session introduces students to the picaresque mode, considering Moll Flanders as a picara. It will discuss formal issues, such as the double narration, and the morality of the novel. Gender and Self-Fashioning. Developing the notion of the picara, this session considers Moll’s crimes as both moral failure and as part of a systemic response to eighteenth-century gender expectations and class constraints. It follows Moll through some of her adventures, situating her within existing crime narratives and probing the place of the women within the novel. Can Moll’s crimes be considered as part of her economic (and gendered) self-fashioning? Front Matter and Generic Expectations. This session will focus on the prefaces and portrait/narrative frontispieces of Oroonoko (1722), Robinson Crusoe (1719) and Moll Flanders (1722), asking how early paratexts operate in terms of authorial self-fashioning, author-character identity, and generic conventions/expectations. Popular Reading: Abridgements and Visual Narrative. This session situates the early novel as “popular” reading; something that was made (in shorter form) available to a wide range of readers. After a brief survey of abridgments and illustrated editions of the Oroonoko, Robinson Crusoe, and Moll Flanders, the session will provide a case study based on Thomas Read’s 1723 abridgement of Moll Flanders. The Epistolary Novel and “writing, to the moment.” Samuel Richardson’s Pamela; or, Virtue Rewarded (1740) is often heralded as the first “proper” novel in English. This session explores how the epistolary novel gives rise to new kinds of realism through description and characterization, and Richardson’s notion of “writing, to the moment.” We will analyze as letter sequence to show how Richardson’s technique creates suspense, intimacy, or moral urgency. Morality and Sentimentality. This session explores the sentimental and didactic nature of Richardson’s novel, considering the audience to whom the novel was addressed, the role of reading, and what it says about class relations in the mid-eighteenth century. Student Presentations Student Presentations Parody/ Essay Workshop. Starting with William Warner’s description of the first years after the publication of Pamela as a “media event,” this session explores some of the critical responses to Richardson’s first novel found in Henry Fielding’s Shamela and Eliza Haywood’s Anti-Pamela. Crucially, this session will investigate how Fielding and Haywood exposed gaps in Richardson’s moral and narrative logic, and Richard’s responsive revisions. “A Comic-Epic Poem in Prose.” While Henry Fielding was prompted to enter the world of fiction in response to Richardson’s Pamela, he did not follow it in terms of mode. This session explores the picaresque nature of The History of Joseph Andrews and of his Friend Mr. Abraham Adams (1742), focusing on his use of the picaro and other stock characters, alongside Field’s own writing on literary character and his self-exclaimed “comic-epic poem in prose.” Interpolated Tales. This session will investigate the use of interpolated tales in Joseph Andrews and consider them against the single unified narrative convention that is traditionally taken to be the hallmark of the novel. Marketing the Novel. This final session looks back over the course, reviewing the developments seen and asking “Did the novel rise?” It also looks forward into the second half of the eighteenth century, considering the canonization, marketing, and illustration of novel.
