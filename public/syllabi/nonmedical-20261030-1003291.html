分析哲学原著选读

2025秋 · PHIL30066.01 · 顾知巍、Chuang LIU(刘闯)

来源：复旦教务系统教学大纲；正文整理。

课程简介（中文）

本课程分为两个部分： 第一部分（第1周至第8周）探讨由虚构实体所引发的本体论与语义学难题——这些并不存在的对象却似乎能够在有意义的言语中发挥作用。课程将研读语言哲学中的经典与当代表述，重点讨论指称、真理、量词化与存在等问题，以及不同理论所承诺的形上学立场。涉及的哲学家包括梅农（Meinong）、罗素（Russell）、奎因（Quine）、克里普克（Kripke）、沃尔顿（Walton）、范·伊万根（van Inwagen）、托马森（Thomasson）等。 第二部分（第9周至第16周）则聚焦于意义的经典哲学理论与自然语言处理（NLP）当代表征方法之间的交汇点，特别是词向量模型（如 word2vec）。学生将深入研读分析哲学中关于语言的奠基性文献——如弗雷格（Frege）、维特根斯坦（Wittgenstein）、戴维森（Davidson）、克里普克（Kripke）、格莱斯（Grice）等人的著作——并批判性地考察这些理论如何与当代计算语言模型的基本假设发生关联或冲突。

课程简介（英文）

This course divides into two part: The first part (week 1-week 8) examines the ontological and semantic puzzles raised by fictional entities—nonexistent objects that nonetheless seem to figure in meaningful discourse. We will engage with classic and contemporary work in the philosophy of language, focusing on issues of reference, truth, quantification, and existence, as well as the metaphysical commitments different theories entail. Authors studied include Meinong, Russell, Quine, Kripke, Walton, van Inwagen, Thomasson, and others. The second part (week 9-week 16) explores the intersection between classic philosophical theories of meaning and contemporary approaches to natural language processing (NLP), with a focus on vector space models such as word2vec. Students will engage deeply with foundational texts in analytic philosophy of language—by Frege, Wittgenstein, Davidson, Kripke, Grice, and others—while critically examining how these theories relate to or challenge the assumptions behind modern computational models of language.

教学目标

本课程旨在探讨虚构实体的语言表达与自然语言处理中的语义建模问题。通过研读经典语言哲学文本与当代语言模型（如 word2vec），学生将理解语言中非实在对象的逻辑地位，掌握分析指称、意义与语境的哲学工具，并具备批判性地评估当代语义模型的能力。

教学内容与进度安排

Week 1: Introduction – The Problem of Non-Being Key Questions: What does it mean to refer to something that doesn’t exist? Can we speak meaningfully about nonexistents? Readings: Stacie Friend, "Fictional characters" (2007) Week 2: Russell, Descriptions, and Logical Form Key Questions: How does Russell’s theory of descriptions solve problems of reference? What are its implications for fictional discourse? Readings: Bertrand Russell, “On Denoting” (1905) Week 3: Quine and the Ontological Commitment Key Questions: Should we posit fictional entities in our ontology? What is the role of quantification in ontological commitment? Readings: W.V.O. Quine, “On What There Is” Amie L. Thomasson, Ontology Made Easy (Chapter 2) Week 4: Kripke, Possible Worlds, and Fictional Names Key Questions: How do fictional names refer, if at all? Can possible worlds semantics account for fictional discourse? Readings: Kripke, “Reference and Existence” (Lectures 1 & 3) Week 5: Fictional Realism and Creationism Key Questions: Are fictional characters real, abstract objects? What kind of existence do they have, if any? Readings: Thomasson, Fiction and Metaphysics (Chapters 1–3) Week 6: Pretense Theory and Make-Believe Key Questions: Is fiction best understood as pretense or make-believe? What is the semantic status of fictional utterances? Readings: Walton, Mimesis as Make-Believe (Chapters 2) Week 7: Pragmatic and Contextual Approaches Key Questions: Can pragmatic theories explain the puzzles of fictional reference? What role does context play in fictional discourse? Readings: David Lewis, “Truth in Fiction” (1978) Week 8: Open Questions and Contemporary Debates Key Questions: What is the best overall theory of fictional entities? How do contemporary philosophers reconcile semantic utility with ontological commitment? Weeks 9–16: Philosophy of Language and word2vec Week 9: Introduction to word2vec and Philosophical Problems of Meaning Technical Reading: Mikolov et al., "Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space" (2013) Goldberg & Levy, “word2vec Explained” (2014) Philosophical Reading: Gottlob Frege, “On Sense and Reference” (1892) Themes: Do vectors capture the "sense" or the "reference" of a word? Week 10: Use-Theories of Meaning and Distributional Semantics Technical Background: The distributional hypothesis: “You shall know a word by the company it keeps” Philosophical Reading: Ludwig Wittgenstein, Philosophical Investigations (§§1–64) Themes: Is word2vec an implementation of a use-theory of meaning? Social externalism and the limitations of distributional methods Week 11: Categories, Prototypes, and Conceptual Spaces Technical Focus: Analogical reasoning in word embeddings (e.g., king – man + woman = queen) Philosophical Reading: Wittgenstein, Philosophical Investigations, §§65–71 (“family resemblance”) Themes: Prototype theory and fuzzy boundaries in concept formation Do embedding spaces reflect conceptual structures or statistical patterns only? Week 12: Truth-Conditional Semantics and Learnability Technical Focus: Can vector models be said to assign truth conditions? Philosophical Reading: Donald Davidson, “Truth and Meaning” (1967) Michael Dummett, Truth and Other Enigmas (selections) Themes: Truth-conditional vs. use-based theories of meaning Are vector spaces compatible with a compositional semantic theory? Week 13: Reference, Polysemy, and Context Technical Focus: Polysemy, contextual embeddings, and disambiguation Philosophical Reading: François Recanati, "Contextualism and Polysemy" (2017) Themes: Can embeddings respect rigid designation? Do models “track” reference, or merely simulate contextual patterns? Week 14: Figurative Language, Metaphor, and Pragmatics Technical Focus: How models capture metaphor, analogy, and non-literal meaning Philosophical Reading: H.P. Grice, “Meaning” (1957); Donald Davidson, “What Metaphors Mean” (1978) Themes: Can statistical models recognize implicature or metaphor? Does analogical behavior (e.g., in vectors) entail semantic understanding? Week 15: Critiques of Semantic Models Readings: Emily Bender & Alexander Koller, “Climbing towards NLU: On Meaning, Form, and Understanding” (ACL 2020) Themes: What does it mean to “understand language”? Are NLP systems semantically interpretable, or just engineering constructs? Week 16: Discussion In-Class Activity: Student-led discussions connecting a philosophical theory to a specific NLP method Final presentations or structured debates Themes: What can philosophy offer to computational semantics? What conceptual frameworks are needed to evaluate language models?
