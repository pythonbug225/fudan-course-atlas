课程简介（中文）

本课程鉴于学时、授课对象认知特点及学习时间，侧重英语诗歌教学。选材兼顾思想性与艺术性，文字优美且富音乐节奏感，便于诵读记忆；以短篇为主，精选雪莱《西风颂》、济慈《希腊古瓮颂》《秋颂》等近代诗人优秀中篇；兼顾诗歌发展历史，以文艺复兴后英美名篇为主，亦含其他国家和地区精品，广受学生欢迎，能引发共鸣、启迪人生。

课程简介（英文）

This English poetry course is carefully planned to fit the amount of class time, students' learning abilities, and their study schedules. The poems are chosen for both their important ideas and their artistic beauty. They feature elegant language and a musical rhythm, making them easier to recite and understand. The course focuses mainly on short poems but also includes famous longer works like Shelley's Ode to the West Wind and Keats' Ode on a Grecian Urn and To Autumn. We trace the history of poetry, focusing on masterpieces from Britain and America after the Renaissance, along with classic poems from around the world. Students have responded very positively to this approach, finding that the material connects with them on a deep, personal level.

教学目标

本课程立足复旦大学“双一流”定位，面向在职研究生开设英语诗歌专题。以经典文本为载体，通过英美诗歌名篇的文学赏析与文化解读，融合价值引领与审美教育，在语言学习中渗透人文精神培育。课程以跨学科视角解析诗歌的思想内核，通过文本细读提升批判性思维，将诗歌的人文价值转化为社会责任感培养，助力学员在提升专业素养的同时，实现跨文化沟通能力与人文情怀的双向进阶，契合高层次人才复合培养目标。

教学方式

本课程以教师精讲为主、课堂提问为辅。教师深入剖析所选经典诗作，系统讲解诗歌语言特色、韵律节奏、创作背景与思想内涵，解析艺术手法与哲学思考，助力在职研究生精准把握诗歌精髓。同时，通过启发式提问，引导学员参与意象解读、情感剖析与主题研讨，激发主动思考与学术思辨，以互动式教学攻克诗歌学习难点，实现文学鉴赏能力与人文素养双提升。

教学内容与进度安排

Week One：Robert Frost Content： • The Road Not Taken • Mending Wall • Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening • Fire and Ice • Acquainted with the Night • Provide, Provide Objectives： Frost is a key figure in American pastoral poetry, known for his accessible language and rural imagery. This lesson helps students grasp the basic forms of poetic language (e.g., rhyme scheme, meter) and introductory analysis methods, emphasizing how his works blend nature with philosophical reflection. Homework： Recite The Road Not Taken. Week Two：Emily Dickinson Content： • I’m Nobody, Who Are You? • Success is Counted Sweetest • A Narrow Fellow in the Grass Objectives： Dickinson’s poetry features concise language and innovative symbolism. Students will learn to analyze poetic imagery (e.g., nature as metaphor) and symbolic techniques, understanding how her unique syntax conveys themes of identity, mortality, and success. Homework： Recite Success is Counted Sweetest. Week Three：Robert Burns Content： • A Red, Red Rose • Auld Lang Syne • My Heart’s in the Highlands Objectives： Introduce students to Scottish romanticism and Burns’ role in folk poetry. Focus on his use of dialect, emotional intensity, and themes of love and patriotism, illustrating how his works embody the romantic emphasis on emotion and nature. Homework： Recite A Red, Red Rose. Week Four：William Wordsworth Content： • I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud • The Solitary Reaper • The World is Too Much with Us Objectives： Wordsworth is a foundational figure of English Romanticism, renowned for his claim that poetry is “the spontaneous overflow of strong emotions recollected in tranquility.” Students will understand his focus on nature, the common man, and the therapeutic power of imagination. Homework： Recite I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud. Week Five：Percy Bysshe Shelley Content： • Ozymandias • Ode to the West Wind Objectives： Explore Shelley’s radical romanticism, emphasizing his use of allegory and natural symbolism (e.g., the west wind as a force of revolution). Analyze how his poetry combines political critique with aesthetic innovation, as seen in the sonnet form of Ozymandias and the rhythmic structure of Ode to the West Wind. Homework： Recite both Ozymandias and Ode to the West Wind. Week Six：John Keats Content： • To Autumn Objectives： Focus on Keats’ “Odes,” which exemplify Romanticism’s sensory richness and meditative tone. Students will analyze his use of tactile imagery (sight, sound, taste), the cyclical theme of nature’s bounty, and the philosophical reflection on time and mortality in To Autumn. Homework： Recite To Autumn. Week Seven：Lord Byron Content： • She Walks in Beauty Objectives： Introduce Byron’s Romantic lyricism, known for its elegance and emotional depth. Explore how She Walks in Beauty uses contrast (light/dark, beauty/virtue) and musical meter to praise both physical and spiritual beauty, reflecting Byron’s iconic “Byronic hero” aesthetic. Homework： Recite She Walks in Beauty. Week Eight：William Butler Yeats Content： • The Lake Isle of Innisfree Objectives： Yeats’ early work embodies Irish Romanticism and his yearning for spiritual escape. Analyze the poem’s pastoral imagery, repetitive rhythm, and symbolism of Innisfree as a utopian ideal, connecting it to his later exploration of mysticism and nationalism. Homework： Recite The Lake Isle of Innisfree. Week Nine：William Shakespeare Content： • Sonnet 18 • Sonnet 29 Objectives： Define the structure and conventions of the Shakespearean sonnet (iambic pentameter, three quatrains + couplet). Explore how Sonnet 18 uses metaphor to immortalize love and Sonnet 29 addresses themes of self-doubt and redemption, showcasing Shakespeare’s mastery of emotional complexity. Homework： Recite both Sonnet 18 and Sonnet 29. Week Ten：Robert Herrick Content： • To the Virgins, to Make Much of Time Objectives： Examine Herrick’s Cavalier poetry, which emphasizes carpe diem (“seize the day”) themes. Analyze the poem’s allegorical use of nature (e.g., roses, sun) to urge youth and enjoyment, reflecting 17th-century humanist attitudes toward life and mortality. Homework： Recite To the Virgins, to Make Much of Time. Week Eleven：Walt Whitman Content： • Captain! My Captain! • I Hear America Singing Objectives： Introduce Whitman’s free verse style, a cornerstone of American modern poetry. Captain! My Captain! uses metaphor to eulogize Abraham Lincoln, while I Hear America Singing celebrates democratic idealism through diverse voices. Discuss how his poetry breaks traditional form to reflect national identity. Homework： Recite Captain! My Captain!. Week Twelve：Carl Sandburg Content： • Chicago Objectives： Explore Sandburg’s urban realism and his role in the Chicago Renaissance. Analyze Chicago’s rough imagery, rhythmic repetition, and paradoxical praise of the city as both “brutal” and “proud,” reflecting early 20th-century industrial America. Homework： Analyze the symbolic use of “Hog Butcher for the World” in Chicago. Week Thirteen：Edwin Arlington Robinson Content： • Richard Cory • Miniver Cheevy Objectives： Robinson’s poems reveal the disillusionment of modern life through ironic storytelling. Richard Cory subverts the myth of wealth and success, while Miniver Cheevy satirizes nostalgia for the past. Discuss how his understated language conveys tragic irony. Homework： Compare the themes of disillusionment in Richard Cory and Miniver Cheevy. Week Fourteen：Ella Wheeler Wilcox & William Henry Davies Content： • Solitude (Wilcox) • Leisure (Davies) Objectives： Examine 19th-century didactic poetry. Solitude explores the law of attraction through aphoristic language, while Leisure critiques modern life’s haste. Contrast their moralizing tones and discuss how each poem uses nature to advocate for mental peace. Homework： Write a short response on whether Leisure remains relevant today. Week Fifteen：Mary Oliver Content： • The Wild Geese • When I Am Among the Trees Objectives： Oliver’s contemporary nature poetry blends simplicity with spiritual insight. Analyze her use of direct address and animal imagery in The Wild Geese, and her meditative tone in When I Am Among the Trees, exploring how she connects human experience to the natural world. Homework： Recite a stanza from The Wild Geese. Week Sixteen：Extended Reading Content： • On Children (Kahlil Gibran) • Desiderata (Max Ehrmann) Objectives： Broaden literary perspectives with cross-cultural works. On Children uses metaphor to discuss parent-child relationships, while Desiderata offers humanist advice. Compare these prose poems to English verse and reflect on universal themes of love, patience, and resilience. Homework： Write a reflection on how Desiderata relates to poetic themes explored in the course.
