十九世纪女性写作

2026春 · FORE70083.01 · Sandro Jung

来源：复旦教务系统教学大纲；正文整理。

课程简介（中文）

本课程将讨论一系列女性作家的小说，并探讨女性作者不仅如何在由男性作家主导的市场中为自己创造成功的角色，同时也如何通过她们的努力，促进了关于女性在社会中角色和权利的日益复杂的讨论。课程以玛丽·沃斯通克拉夫特的《女性权利辩护》（1792）为背景开篇，分析简·奥斯汀、安妮与夏洛特·勃朗特、伊丽莎白·盖斯凯尔、玛丽·布拉登以及弗朗西斯·霍奇森·伯内特等作家如何构建不同的女性身份与道德观念，如何赋予其作品中的女性角色力量，以及她们如何展现女性作为性别化社会中的参与者。课程还将关注诸如“多余女性”问题，这一问题在关于女性如何在传统婚姻单位之外谋生的辩论中占据了主导地位。

课程简介（英文）

This course will discuss a range of novels by women writers and investigate how female authors not only fashioned successful roles for themselves in a marketplace that was dominated by male writers but also how their efforts contributed to an increasingly complex discussion of the role and rights a woman held within society. Opening with a contextualisation of Mary Wollstonecraft’s Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792), the course will analyse in which ways authors such as Jane Austen, Anne and Charlotte Brontë, Elizabeth Gaskell, Mary Braddon, and Frances Hodgson Burnett construct different notions of female identity and morality, how they empower the female characters they introduce in their works, and how they portray them as participants in a gendered society. The course will pay attention to issues such as the Redundant Woman Question, which came to assume a dominant place in debates regarding how women were supposed to make a living outside of the traditional marriage unit, and consider the portrayal of marginal female figures (including the femme fatale and the female child) in fiction.

教学目标

The course aims to introduce students to novels by some of the major female writers of the nineteenth century. Not only will it furnish students with accounts of how the works produced by these novelists need to be understood in their socio-cultural contexts of production and consumption. But students will also learn about how, in the light of feminist critique, these authors need to be understood as having made significant contributions to the enfranchisement of women in the period. Students will learn about the different kinds of generic types of novels – ranging from the novel of manners, social problem fiction, and the Bildungsroman to the sensational novel and novels for children – in the process garnering a better understanding of different versions of realism, including how different character types such as the detective or the femme fatale were shaped by the works to be discussed. A focus of the course will be on how the different works were read and understood by their contemporaries and how these readings have affected their modern reception.

教学方式

lectures and seminars

教学内容与进度安排

1 Introduction Aims of the course, including assessment; writing by and about women; the woman in literature and society at the end of the eighteenth century. Mary Wollstonecraft’s Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792) and Proto-Feminism; excerpt from Jane Austen’s Sense and Sensibility (1811) to be discussed in light of Wollstonecraft’s treatise. Learning outcomes: Students will be familiarized with the central challenges that women, because of their gender, faced in late eighteenth- and early nineteenth-century society. The study of Wollstonecraft’s work and the extract from Sense and Sensibility will demonstrate the performativity of gender and the ways in which these issues were addressed by Austen’s characters. 2 Charlotte Brontë’s Villette (1853): Types of Female Characters and Emancipation through Professional Work Learning outcomes: Students will gain an understanding of the sociological characterization of Lucy Snowe, as well as how women of her class were expected to function in Victorian society. A particular focus on class will establish the fundamental difference between Austen’s and Brontë’s female characters. This session will also define what emancipation meant for women in the 1840s and 1850s and how enfranchisement needs to be understood historically. Students will also be able to probe how intercultural constructions of feminine morality are offered to readers in the context of Lucy’s work at the Pensionnat. This session will be the first to draw on feminist critique as a tool that needs to be defined by historical contextualization. 3 Charlotte Brontë’s Female Teachers This session will study the characterization of different teachers and the ways in which they discharge their duties. A list of essay/presentation topics will be made available in this session. Learning outcomes: Students will be able to identify the uniqueness of Lucy as a teacher through a comparison of other teacher figures in the novel. They will also be able to contextualize Lucy in terms of contemporaneous reviews of the novel and rationalize why Lucy’s character was largely considered unsuitable as a teacher – a period judgment that clashes with modern accounts of her. 4 Anne Brontë’s The Tenant of Wildfell Hall (1848) and the Question of Female Independence This session will focus on the married female protagonist of Brontë’s most famous novel, concentrating on the analysis of how Helen Graham struggles to lead a life of morality while her debauched husband sets the worst possible example but still retains legal power over her to control her assets and personal movements. Her decision to leave her husband’s household and to lead a life as a single woman will be probed in light of a consideration of whether this move really results in independence for her. Learning Outcomes: Students will learn about the working mechanisms of marital law in the nineteenth century, as well as in what ways the way of life Helen Graham adopts once she leaves her husband’s home provoked an ambivalent reception of the protagonist. Helen’s character will be related to the Victorian notion of the Angel of the House, and students will be able to assess in what ways Helen is cast as a conventional Victorian married woman. 5 The Tenant of Wildfell Hall and the Role of the Married Mother Learning Outcomes: Married mothers feature rarely as protagonists in Victorian fiction. Students will, therefore, pay particular attention to how Helen Graham’s motherhood is portrayed, as well as how the status of supposed widow complicates her future relationship with Gilbert Markham. Next to the role of the mother, attention will be paid to how Brontë conceived of widowhood and the role that widows could occupy in society. By the end of this session, students will have immersed themselves, through the contextualization of issues related to motherhood and widowhood, in the gender-defined textual world of The Tenant of Wildfell Hall and have an informed understanding of the social concerns at stake in the construction of Helen Graham. 6 Elizabeth Gaskell’s Mary Barton (1848): Types of Female Characters Learning outcomes: Students will continue their exploration of different female characters, as well as how their class shapes them, focusing on a different kind of work that Gaskell introduces in her factory novel. They will also be able to differentiate the different kinds of realism and characterization deployed by Austen, the Brontës, and Gaskell, as well as learn about some stereotypical character constellations that frequently recur in Victorian fiction. 7 Working-Class Femininity, Morality, and the Dangers of Love Learning outcomes: By the time students discuss Gaskell’s female characters, they will already have encountered upper-class and middle-class female characters and will thereby be able to establish how different moral codes and social expectations were applied to women of different classes in the period. Students will be introduced to three different kinds of characters, the rebel (Mary), the angel, and the prostitute (Esther) and assess how each faces temptations and longings that need to be resisted but frequently are not. Love as a sentimental concept will be problematized in light of the strict Victorian moral code for the working classes. 8 Female Writers on One Another: Gaskell writing about Charlotte Brontë This session will introduce students to a number of extracts from Gaskell’s biography of Charlotte Brontë and examine the narrative and rhetorical strategies Gaskell uses to produce an influential characterization of Brontë. This account will be complemented by other biographical notices of Brontë that appeared towards the end of her life and shortly after her death. Learning outcomes: Students will be able to compare how the construction of femininity in works of fiction correlates with the discourses of identity, morality, and female selfhood in the biographical writings of Gaskell and Charlotte Brontë and especially how Gaskell constructed a version of Brontë for posterity. Students will learn about how considerations of the literary marketplace, including the building of a marketable reputation as a bestselling novelist, induced Gaskell to produce a one-sided account of Brontë which highlighted the author of the biography with essential qualities the Yorkshire novelist was supposed to lack. 9 The Redundant Woman Question Learning outcomes: Students will be provided with a range of short extracts to sketch the redundant woman question by which various societies and the British government sought to promote the emigration of single women to the colonies in order to spread imperial ideology and reduce the significant surplus of unmarried women in Britain. On the basis of woman characters in the works studied who failed to find marriage partners, students will be able to consider the way of life of women who remained single and who, with increasing age, became peripheral to a society that championed the family as ideal micro-community. 10 Mary Braddon’s Lady Audley’s Secret (1862) and Victorian Sensationalism Learning outcomes: While previous sessions of this course introduced students to realist and social problem fiction, this session introduces sensation fiction by focusing on the mystery of Lady Audley. Students will study the characterization of the novel’s protagonist as part of the vogue for sensational writing in which transgressive woman characters commit crimes not only against individuals but also against the moral standards of the time. The novel will be introduced as an example of detective but also of mystery fiction in which Lady Audley is cast as a chameleon capable of adapting to circumstances in a way Victorian women were not supposed to be. 11 Victorian Morality and the Femme Fatale Learning outcomes: In this session, students will be able to compare how Wilkie Collins (in his character of Lydia Gwilt) and Braddon fashion femme fatales that are conceptualized as instances of moral others in the Victorian period. They will learn how these figures’ identity of difference distinguish them from such a character as Esther in Mary Barton and how reviewers frequently associated the femme fatale with notions of madness. 12 15-minute student presentations 13 15-minute student presentations 14 Frances Hodgson Burnett’s A Little Princess (1905) and the Female Child Learning outcomes: This session will introduce a pre-adolescent female protagonist and chart her social decline from colonial heiress to servant as well as her rise to fortune again as part of the pedagogical programme of Burnett. Students will encounter a large spectrum of female characters whose personalities will need to be tested against the norms of morality and femininity advanced by novels studied earlier. Sara Crewe, even though a child character, represents a model of charitable that adults are encouraged to emulate. 15 Sara Crewe as Surrogate Mother Learning outcomes: The focus of this session will enable students to recognize a strong shift in terms of ideal motherhood that is only rarely spelt out in early Victorian fiction. Sara’s maturity will be read as intrinsic, as having been refined by her upbringing, affording those in need the support and charity they deserve. Students will be able to identify a shift towards a romanticization of young mother figures in children’s fiction that is largely absent in earlier Victorian fiction for adults. 16 Women in Victorian society and their modes of self-construction Learning outcomes: This final session of the course will be devoted to a discussion of central issues regarding the constructions of femininity and female selfhood in the novels studied, as well as to how the study of gender offers greater insight into the workings of different social classes in the Victorian period.
