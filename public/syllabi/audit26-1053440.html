课程简介（中文）

本短期研究生课程旨在介绍如何在经济学研究中将文本作为数据使用。当前，越来越多的经济活动——包括新闻资讯、政策与央行沟通、企业信息披露、社交媒体等——以非结构化的文本形式记录，这些文本可以被转化为关于情绪、预期、不确定性、关注度及其他研究变量的量化指标。本课程遵循 Gentzkow、Kelly 和 Taddy（2019）提出的分析框架，涵盖完整的研究流程：文本的数值化表示、潜在属性的预测，以及将所得的量化指标应用于描述性分析和因果分析。课程中讲授的方法从基于词频统计和词典法出发，逐步推进到带面板的文本回归，直至将大语言模型（LLMs）作为测量工具加以运用。每个主题均配有动手实践的 Python 实验环节，而近期经济学研究中的前沿应用案例则帮助学生认识其中的机遇以及可能存在的问题，如测量误差、样本选择偏误和验证问题。学生将需要自主提出、提交、修改并汇报一份运用上述方法开展经济学研究的原创性研究计划。

课程简介（英文）

This short graduate course introduces the use of text as data in economic research. A growing share of economic activity – news, policy and central-bank communication, corporate disclosures, social media – is recorded as unstructured text that can be turned into quantitative measures of sentiment, expectations, uncertainty, attention and other measures of interest. Following the framework of Gentzkow, Kelly and Taddy (2019), it covers the full workflow: representing text numerically, predicting latent attributes, and using the resulting measures in descriptive and causal analysis. Methods progress from count-based and dictionary approaches through penalised text regression to large language models (LLMs) as measurement tools. Hands-on Python labs accompany each topic, and frontier applications from recent economics research highlight both opportunities and pitfalls like measurement error, selection and validation. Students develop, submit, revise and present an original research proposal applying these methods.

教学目标

By the end of the course, students will be able to: (1) explain what distinguishes text from conventional economic data and judge when text-based measurement is appropriate; (2) implement the core text-as-data workflow in Python—from preprocessing and representation to dictionary methods, penalised text regression and the use of large language models as measurement tools; (3) critically assess validity, measurement error, selection, reproducibility and the responsible use of text and AI tools in research; and (4) design, write and orally defend an original research proposal that applies these methods to an economic question.

教学方式

The course combines interactive lectures with hands-on coding classes. The longer Monday sessions introduce concepts, methods and economic applications; shorter Tuesday sessions are Python labs in which students apply each method to real text corpora. Methods are taught through worked examples and recent papers. Learning is consolidated through an individual or small-group research proposal that students draft, submit for written feedback, revise and present for in-class discussion. The language of instruction is English, all coding is in Python.

教学内容与进度安排

Preliminary, may be adjusted

Week 1 · Mon 7 Sept, 18:30–21:05 — Foundations: Text as Data in Economics

Content: what makes text distinctive as data; the economic constructs text can capture (sentiment, expectations, uncertainty, attention, etc.) and the value of real-time, high-frequency measurement; key risks (measurement error and ambiguity, selection in who produces text, temporal and topical drift, model opacity, ethics and privacy); the Gentzkow–Kelly–Taddy workflow (represent text as a numerical array, predict latent attributes, use the resulting measures in descriptive or causal analysis); dimensionality reduction as the central problem; and a survey of landmark economic applications.

Week 1 · Tue 8 Sept, 08:00–09:40 — Lab 1 — From Raw Text to a Document–Term Matrix (Python)

Content: sourcing text (APIs, web scraping, archives/OCR) and legal and ethical considerations; the preprocessing pipeline (tokenisation, stop-word removal, stemming vs lemmatisation, pruning rare and frequent terms); bag-of-words, n-grams and TF-IDF; constructing and inspecting a document–term matrix; setting up a reproducible Python environment

Week 2 · Mon 14 Sept, 18:30–21:05 — Dictionary Methods and Supervised Text Regression

Content: dictionary and lexicon methods (General Inquirer/Harvard-IV, Loughran–McDonald, LIWC), constructing and validating custom dictionaries, and their limitations (context, negation, domain dependence); the high-dimensional prediction problem and the bias–variance trade-off; penalised regression (Lasso, Ridge, elastic net) and logistic classifiers; train–validation–test splits, cross-validation and performance metrics. Applications include Tetlock (2007), Loughran–McDonald (2011), Baker–Bloom–Davis (2016) and Gentzkow–Shapiro–Taddy (2019).

Week 2 · Tue 15 Sept, 08:00–09:40 — Lab 2 — Measuring a Construct (Python)

Content: build a sentiment/uncertainty measure with a dictionary; train a supervised classifier on labelled data; compare the two approaches; validate against hand-coded labels and assess out-of-sample performance.

Week 3 · Mon 21 Sept, 18:30–21:05 — Large Language Models for Economic Research

Content: bridge from static word vectors to contextual representations, then generative large language models (LLMs); using LLMs as measurement instruments (zero- and few-shot classification, annotation, information extraction); prompt design; reliability, validation against human labels, reproducibility and cost; ethical and practical considerations. Two frontier case studies from current research: an LLM-based meta-analysis of EU cohesion-policy evaluations (Asatryan, Birkholz and Heinemann, 2025) and the effect of media on household inflation expectations (Bachmann and Birkholz).

Week 3 · Tue 22 Sept, 08:00–09:40 — Lab 3 — Using LLMs for Measurement (Python)

Content: apply an LLM (via an API) to a classification, annotation or extraction task on an economic corpus; design and iterate prompts; validate LLM outputs against human labels; and document the workflow for reproducibility.

Week 4 · Mon 28 Sept, 18:30–21:05 — Research-Proposal Presentations, Discussion and Synthesis

Content: students present their individual or small-group research proposals applying text-as-data methods, followed by structured peer and instructor discussion
