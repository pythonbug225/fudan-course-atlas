英美儿童文学

2025秋 · FORE70084.01 · Russell Benjamin Palmer

来源：复旦教务系统教学大纲；正文整理。

课程简介（中文）

本课程采用基于媒体技术的方法，向学生介绍历史上的儿童文学及其现代变体，因此符合国家的新文科愿景。通过探讨影响儿童文学发展的印刷文化和意识形态发展，该课程鼓励学生将技术发展视为文学史的一部分。 课程将涵盖儿童文学从十八世纪中叶开始到第一个儿童文学黄金时代（1865-1915 年）结束的发展历程。.

课程简介（英文）

This course introduces students to historical children’s literature and its modern-day incarnations by taking a media-technology based approach, aligning with the national vision of the New Liberal Arts. By addressing print-cultural and ideological developments that influenced the development of children’s literature, the course encourages students to consider technological developments as part of the history of literature. The course will cover the development of children’s literature from its beginnings in the mid-eighteenth century to the end of the first Golden Age of children’s literature (1865-1915) in Britain and the United States. It takes in a wide range of genres, covering children’s verse, prose narrative, and drama. Major works covered include The History of Little Goody Two-Shoes, Louisa May Alcott’s Little Women, L. Frank Baum’s The Wonderful Wizard of Oz, and James Barrie’s Peter Pan.

教学目标

The primary purpose of the course will be to situate works written for children within the socio-cultural and print-cultural contexts in which they were produced and consumed, enabling students to learn about how authors and publishers produced works for children. Students will learn about genres produced for children and the role of illustrations in children’s books, as well as the business and the marketing influences that made children’s books look a certain way, thereby conditioning a child’s understanding of a text and literature more generally. The course will also consider how works were adapted and transmediated by later generations using newer media technologies as integral parts of the history of children’s literature. As such, this course will pay attention to the printed book and illustrations, and the processes of adaptation for other media and genres (e.g. film adaptation and novelization).

教学方式

This course is designed to be interactive, creating a dynamic teaching situation. Students are encouraged to engage in discussions and ask questions. The course introduces students to the relevance of print culture and performance to children’s reading activities, as well as to the marketing children’s books. In addition, the course embraces extended notions of textuality and literature, examining how children’s classics are reworked over generations verbally, visually, and medially. As such, media and adaptation play a central role in the course; students will examine multiple editions of works, film adaptations, dramatic performances, and novelization. Teaching methods include lectures, discussion and in-class performances.

教学内容与进度安排

Week 1. Amusement and Instruction This session investigates the beginnings of what is considered children’s literature proper, understand its origins and also how it different from previous romances. Key developments rest of Lockean ideas and print cultural and market advances. Week 2. Multi-Modal Reading The visual components of children’s books were important for didactic as well as interpretive purposes. This session focuses on the multiple roles of illustration and page design through early editions of The History of Little Goody Two-Shoes, explaining how, through extended notions of textuality, we can see their role in storytelling, characterization, and narrative progression. Reading Assignment: Part 1 of The History of Little Goody Two-Shoes Suggested Reading: Palmer, Russell "Graphic Characterization and Visual Narrative in The History of Little Goody Two-Shoes," Foreign Literature Studies, vol. 46, no. 6 (2024): 12-26. Week 3. Adapting Little Goody Two-Shoes: Ideology and Print Technology The History of Little Goody Two-Shoes was a major success for generations in Britain and America. This class explores some of the ways in which the story was adapted for different readers and the ways in which printed technologies impacted the changes. Reading Assignment: Part 2 of The History of Little Goody Two-Shoes Week 4. Children Reading Poetry Poetry was an essential literary genre for young readers in the eighteenth and early-nineteenth centuries. This session explores the pedagogical and social ideas surrounding children and poetry by considering poetry especially written for children and the adaptation of pre-existing poems. Reading Assignment: Isaac Watt, "Against Idleness and Mischief" (1715); "Children in the Wood" (1595); and "The Wild Boar and the Ram” from John Gay’s Fables (1727, 1738) Suggested Reading: Louise Joy. Literature’s Children: The Critical Child and the Art of Idealization (London: Bloomsbury, 2019), pp. 63-83. Week 5. Nature, Ethics, and Middle-Class Female Writers From the 1780s, much children’s literature started to present a more starkly moralistic outlook, due to the growing influence of Evangelists and the development of the Sunday School movement. This session considers the works of Mary Ann and Dorothy Kilner and Sarah Trimmer, whose works for children were published by the prominent publisher John Marshall. Extracts from the Trimmer’s Fabulous Histories. Designed for the Instruction of Children Respecting their Treatment of Animals will be used to contrast ideas and ethics concerning animals with John Newbery’s earlier children’s fiction. Reading Assignment: Extracts from Sarah Trimmer's Fabulous Histories (1786) Week 6. Domestic Realism Written specifically as book for girls, Alcott’s Little Women has become a classic that endures till this day, but it is also infused with mid nineteenth-century instruction regarding normative female roles. This session examines how Alcott uses an omniscient narrator as a vehicle of moral commentary. Students will be able to explain the role of Alcott’s moralizing interventions and their absence in modern adaptations. Reading Assignment: Chapers 1 & 2 from Louisa May Alcott’s Little Women (1868 & 1869) Week 7. Jo March This session focuses on the character of Jo March, considering not only the character originated by Alcott, but also how the character has been modified and adapted in film and TV adaptations. Reading Assignment: Chapters 8 & 15 from Louisa May Alcott's Little Women (1868 & 1869) Week 8. Innocence and Nonsense The legacy of earlier nineteenth-century notions of childhood are visible in poetry written for children into the mid-Victoria period. This session examines the connection between childhood and innocence developed in the poetry of Romantic poets and echoed in the much later poetry of Robert Louis Stevenson in his popular A Child's Garden of Verses (1885). It also considers the play of nonsense poetry in children's literature. Reading Assignment: Excerpts from Robert Louis Stevenson, A Child's Garden of Verses (1885) Lewis Carroll's "The Jabberwocky" (1872) Weeks 9 & 10. Student Presentations Week 11. Children’s Theatre and Performance Theatres have not always been seen as appropriate places for children. This session examines the debate surrounding child, theatres, and child actors, as well juvenile dramatic performances in schools and in the home, as a prelude to the development of professional theatre for children. First performed in 1904, J. M. Barrie’s Peter Pan will be examined as a landmark in this development. The final session focuses on Barrie's "lost boys," who represented for children an exciting and adventurous existence, devoid of adult supervision; but for adults, ungoverned children were a cause for societal anxiety. Reading Assignment: Acts 1-3 from J. M. Barrie, Peter Pan (1928) Suggested Reading: Marah Gubar, "Peter Pan as Children's Theatre: The Issue of Audience" (2011) Week 12. Lost Boys: Peter Pan and Social Criticism This class focuses on Barrie’s “lost boys,” who represented for children an exciting and adventurous existence, devoid of adult supervision; but for adults, ungoverned children were a cause for societal anxiety. Reading Assignment: Acts 4 & 5 from J. M. Barrie, Peter Pan (1911) Week 13. Novelization Before publishing the fixed play script, Barrie re-wrote the story of Peter Pan the play into Peter Pan and Wendy, a novel. This class investigates the process of novelization through Barries creations, comparing the character and plot, and how elements have been adapted for a novel format. It also looks forward to a recent example of novelization based on the film Wonka. Reading Assignment: Excerpts from J. M. Barrie, Peter and Wendy (1911) Suggest Reading:Jan Baetens, Novelization (Ohio University Press, 2018) Week 14. A Modern Fairy Tale: The Wonderful Wizard of Oz This session identifies the fairy tale elements of the novel before considering it as a development of the children’s fantasy novel. It will be situated within the socio-cultural context of late nineteenth-century America and interrogated for nationally-specific elements. Reading Assignment: Excerpts from L. Frank Baum, The Wonderful Wizard of Oz (1900) Week 15. Cinematic Legend: The Wizard of Oz (1939) This session explores thematic and media-specific elements of the MGM film The Wizard of Oz. In particular, it will question how the new media required narrative changes and addition in order to appeal to child audiences. Viewing Assignment: Watch the MGM film The Wizard of Oz. Suggested Reading: Benjamin Sears, "'The Road to Oz': From Book to Movie Musical," in Adapting The Wizard of Oz (2019): 27-52. Week 16. Course Review and Essay Workshop This final session reviews key elements of the course and provides help for students writing their final graded essays.
