来源：复旦教务系统教学大纲；正文整理。

课程简介（中文）

本课程旨在为数字原住民提供英国文学史概览，同时符合新文科的主要原则。媒体形式和技术在文学发展和传播中的作用被融入到每一堂课中。因此，本课程在以读者为中心的新研究发展与以作者为中心的传统课程之间取得了平衡。该课程从英国文学的各个主要时期一直到当代，选取了大量的文学作品。课程涵盖英国戏剧、诗歌和小说。

课程简介（英文）

This course offers an overview of English literary history for digital natives, while conforming to the main principles of the New Liberal Arts. The role of media forms and technologies in the development and dissemination of literature is integrated into each class. The course, therefore, balances newer reader-focused research developments with traditional author-focused sessions. The course samples a wide range of literary works from all major periods of English literature up to the present day. Examining works written in prose and verse, the course covers drama, poetry, and fiction.

教学目标

After completing this course, students will be able to name and explain the main literary periods in English history in a basic manner. They will be able to relate literary periods to key ideas, literary forms, and authors. In addition, they will be able to articulate how literature and technology intertwine historically and today. The course follows a chronological order, although themes such as orality, print culture, and performances reoccur throughout. It balances the inclusion of major canonical works by both men - Chaucer’s The Canterbury Tales, Shakespeare’s Macbeth, and Defoe’s Robinson Crusoe - and women - Mary Shelly’s Frankenstein, Christina Rosetti’s “Goblin Market,” and Virginia Woolf’s Mrs. Dalloway. The course ends by coming up to date, examining performance poetry and the role of AI in literary creation. Digital Literary Timeline Project. Students design and build a digital timeline of English literary history based on 4-5 of the course texts and on specific media forms. Each timeline should have an overall theme. Work from A1 and A2 may be reused and integrated into the timeline. The project comprises several stages (see Assessment).

教学方式

This course is designed to highlight the relationship between English literature and the media technologies through which it is realized. Departing from the traditional lecture course, each class integrates “Media Focus” and “Digital World” slots: the former introduces media forms pertinent to the text and period being taught; the latter introduces digital tools for literary students. While many classes integrate AI-based exercises, the focus is on using AI as a tool for learning about literature and literary history, not a quick route to the answer. Assessment for this course is designed to enable students to learn and practice core literary skills, while drawing on their own creativity and applying their digital knowledge. Traditional readings in literary history and of key literary texts are paired with experimental digital projects. Set readings from the course textbook are supported by online quizzes.

教学内容与进度安排

Week 1. Medieval Performance This introductory session introduced to the core aims and objectives of the course, as well as the forms of assessment. The second half of the class will discuss medieval morality plays and consider the roles of performance and orality in medieval English culture. Text: Everyman Textbook: No reading Media Focus: Performance and Orality Digital World: Assessment Explanation Week 2. Chaucer: Voice & Manuscript Geoffrey Chaucer is one of the most famous names in English literary history and is often regarded as the “father of English poetry.” This session examines his works, focusing on The Canterbury Tales, in addition to early forms of literary transmission and reading. Text: Excerpts from the “General Prologue” in Chaucer’s The Canterbury Tales (1397) Textbook: “Chapter 2. Middle English Literature,” esp. pp. 11-13 & 17-25. Media Focus: Scribal culture, oral delivery Digital World: AI translation of Middle English. What are the gains/losses compared to scholarly notes and glosses? Week 3. Renaissance Epic: Print & Allegory The impact of Edmund Spenser’s poetry was felt for centuries, as later generations of poets attempted to imitate his style. This session takes a brief look at Spenser’s The Faerie Queene, an epic poem (a long, narrative poem), contextualizing it within the court of Elizabeth I and analyzing its use of allegory. Text: Excerpts from Edmund Spenser’s The Fairie Queene (1590) Textbook: “Chapter 3. Sixteenth-Century Poetry and Prose,” esp. pp. 26-28 & 37-40, but not pp. 32-37. Media Focus: Print Technology, Illustrations Digital World: AI translation and textual analysis / Generate AI image of allegorical scene → critique accuracy Week 4. Shakespeare: Drama and Poetry This session is devoted to William Shakespeare, one of the greatest playwrights in World Literature. Students will examine key elements of tragedy through Macbeth and become acquainted with the themes and form of a Shakespearean sonnet. Texts: Excepts from Macbeth (1605) and “Sonnet 64” Textbook: “Chapter 3. Sixteenth-Century Poetry and Prose,” pp. 32-37; “Chapter 4. Shakespeare,” esp. pp. 41-42 & 47-52. Media Focus: The First Folio edition (1623) and Shakespeare’s legacy Digital World: Use AI to “modernize” a soliloquy from Macbeth. Analyze the changes in tone. Week 5. Enlightenment Fiction and Paratexts Although the eighteenth-century writers produced beautiful poetry and a mass of comic and satirical plays, the era is most associated with the beginnings of the English novel. This session examines one of the first novels written in English, which is also one of the most famous today: Daniel Defoe’s Robinson Crusoe. Text: Robinson Crusoe (1719) Textbook: “Chapter 8. The Novel: The First Hundred Years,” pp. 102-109. Media Focus: Title Pages and Frontispieces Digital World: Imaging Friday Timeline Project Theme: Brainstorming common motifs (theme) Due: AI Translation Assignment Week 6. Romantic Poetry: Print Authenticity This session provides a brief introduction to some of the key elements of British Romanticism. Focusing on poems by William Wordsworth and Percy Bysshe Shelley, students will begin to understand what Romantic poets were writing against, their perception of “nature,” and how the began to reshape English poetry through the example of Lyrical Ballads (1798/1800). Texts: William Wordsworth’s “We Are Seven” (1798) and “The Leach Gatherer” (1807); 2 stanzas of Percy Bysshe Shelley’s “Mont Blanc” (1817) Textbook: “Chapter 9. The Romantic Period,” esp. pp. 115-125. Media Focus: The Revision of Lyrical Ballads Digital World: Co-write a nature poem with AI; edit for “voice.” Or find different digitized editions and compare? Timeline Project: Specifying media forms and technologies (theme) Week 7. Gothic Fiction: Science, Anxiety and Form Mary Shelley an important early English female novelist. While her story of Frankenstein is well known today, this session will ask why it is important in terms of literary history and gothic conventions. Text: Excerpts from Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus (1818) Textbook: “Chapter 8. The Novel: The First Hundred Years,” pp. 110-112. Media Focus: Novel Structure, 1818 edition Digital World: Building a digital timeline (idea sharing) Timeline Project: Pitch (including a thesis) Due: Digital Timeline Proposal Week 8. Victorian Serial Fiction Like many works of fiction we think of today as “novels,” Charles Dicken’s Oliver Twist was originally published in installments in the magazine Bentley's Miscellany. This session surveys Victorian works published serially, the significance of periodicals in the literary history, and some formal differences of writing in installments. Text: An excerpt from Charles Dicken’s Oliver Twist; or, The Parish Boy's Progress (1837-1839) Textbook: “Chapter 10. Victorian Literature, 1837-1857,” esp. pp. 128-135, but not pp. 138-142. Media Focus: Serial publication (periodicals and magazines) Digital World: Use AI to generate a 1-sentence cliffhanger for the end of the first installment of Oliver Twist. Compare the result to techniques used by Dickens. Week 9. Victorian Poetry: Image & Market Successful Victorian poetry was frequently printed many times in different collections and anthologies. Illustrations functioned not only as interpretive decoration, but were used by publishers to market and sell their editions. This session contrast 2 very successful poems by famous Victorian poets: Lord, Alfred Tennyson and Christina Rosetti. Texts: Alfred, Lord Tennyson’s “Lady of Shallot” (1832/1842) and excerpts from Christina Rossetti’s “Goblin Market: A Tale of Two Sisters” (1862) Textbook: “Chapter 10. Victorian Literature, 1837-1857,” pp. 138-142; “Chapter 11. Victorian Literature, 1857-1876,” esp. pp. 153-156. Media Focus: Illustration Digital World: Generate AI images for a stanza. Analyze how well the images fit. Week 10. Victorian Melodrama and Spectacle This session investigates Victorian theatre, focusing on melodrama. As the most popular form of theatre at the time, audiences range widely as drama become accessible to an ever-widening social groups. Text: Excerpts from Oscar Wilde’s The Importance of Being Earnest (1899) Textbook: “Chapter 12. Victorian Literature, 1876-1901,” esp. pp. 167-169. Media Focus: Victorian Theatre Performance / published plays Digital World: Satirizing Theatre Week 11. Modernism: Fragmentation & Typography Virginia Woolf was one of the major exponents of “stream of consciousness” fiction. In this session, student learn why her work and fragmentation are specifically modernist. Text: An excerpt from Virginia Woolf’s Dalloway (1925) Textbook: “Chapter 13. The Early Years,” p. 170; “Chapter 14. The Twentieth Century: Between the Wars,” esp. pp. 195-198. Media Focus: The Hogarth Press and use of typography Digital World: Feed a fragmented passage into AI. Assess it for coherence loss. Week 12. Post-WWII Allegory and Media Reception This session engages with themes that cut across works produced after World War II, moving into the Cold War era. These include social critique and a growing number of dystopian works. Text: Excerpts from William Golding’s Lord of the Flies (1956) Textbook: “Chapter 15. The Twentieth Century: The Second World War to the end of the Millennium,” esp. pp. 202-205 & 209-213. Media Focus: Movie adaptation (1963). Compare the “reader” experience of the novel and film. Digital World: Generate AI summaries of Lord of the Flies in the style of a 1950s and a 1990s British newspaper. Analyze for genre expectations and themes. Due: Media & Reception Assignment Week 13. Post-1980s Literature This session explores late 20th-century writing and writers, as the idea of who counts as British also expands with the kinds of creative output regarded as “literature.” Dub poetry will form the case study for this week. Texts: Benjamin Zephaniah’s “Dis Poetry” (1992) and “Us and Dem” (1990) Textbook: “Chapter 16. Postscript,” pp. 217-220. Media Focus: Performance Poetry (live vs. recording) Digital World: How does recording technology (vinyl, digital) shape the reception and meaning of performance poetry? Week 14. Project Workshop This session is devoted to discussing student’s Digital Timelines. Students will informally present their Digital Timelines and exchange ideas regarding implementation. Students will also receive feedback in the literary and media content, giving time for revision before the final deadline. Due: Digital Timeline Artifact (draft) Week 15. Digital Futures Digital technologies, especially AI, have in recent years captured the imagination of authors. This session examines AI narrators and AI characters through AI-POV passages in Kazou Ishiguro’s Klara and the Sun. Finally, this session also consider AI-authorship, human-AI co-creation and the future of English literature. Text: Excerpts from Kazuo Ishiguro’s Klara and the Sun (2021) Textbook: No chapter Media Focus: Digital books Digital World: Literature and deepfakes (AI narration) Week 16. Course Review In this final session, we revisit some of the main points of the course and reflect on what has been learned. Text: No reading assignment Textbook: Evaluation of textbook Media Focus: Media Timeline Recap Digital Focus: What other, online or app-based resources my supplement or replace a textbook? Why? Due: Final Digital Timeline Artifact & Written Paper

考核构成

  • 课堂表现Participation 5.0% Quizzes (5%). Each quiz consists of up to 10 multiple-choice questions based on the set readings from the course textbook. Students have 2 attempts to answer each question correctly, as the focus of the quizzes is learning.
  • 作业/实验/实践Assignment 40.0% AI Translation (20%). In this task, students build on in-class experience of using AI to mediate medieval and early modern literary texts. Students must “modernize” a short passage (5-10 lines) from either Spenser’s The Faeire Queene or Shakespeare’s Macbeth. a course text from Weeks 1-4, identifying key differences between the original and the AI output, and reflecting on how AI alters the original in terms of tone, use of literary devices, and cultural/historical specificity. Approximately 500 words, plus original passage and AI translation. Media & Reception (20%). Students examine how a specific media form shaped the structure of a text and/or the original readers’ understanding of a text studied in weeks 8-11. Approximately 500 words.
  • 汇报Presentation 10.0% Digital Timeline Project Proposal (10%). Students should submit a 1-page proposal for their Timeline, indicating the overall theme, which course texts will be selected, a preliminary scholarly argument (a few sentences only), a tentative description of the artifact and Tech Stack, and potential challenges.
  • 课题研讨Topic Discussion 20.0% Digital Artifact (20%). Students create the timeline using their choice of digital technology/apps. Each text should have an entry (max. 100 words) explaining how it is typical of the period, the role of media in its production/reception, and why it is important for the chosen theme, plus one image.
  • 课题论文Research Paper 25.0% Written Paper (25%). Students should write a paper based on their themed timeline that contains 4 sections: An Introduction (150 words); Period Analyses (600-750 words); a Synthesis of cross-ear patterns (300 words); and Critical Reflection (300 words), in which students explain how they critically engaged with AI/Digital technology through validation and critique, and what they learned about literary history through the process of building a digital timeline.
