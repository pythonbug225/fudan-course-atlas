文学、调适和改编

2025秋 · FORE70085.01 · Sandro Jung

来源：复旦教务系统教学大纲；正文整理。

课程简介（中文）

本课程将介绍一个前沿的跨学科研究领域，专注于不同媒介（除了传统的书籍或手稿文本技术以及更现代的阅读平台）如何影响文学作品的理解：跨媒介文学研究。

课程简介（英文）

This course will introduce a cutting-edge area of interdisciplinary research on literature that focuses on how different media – apart from the traditional text technologies of the book or manuscript, as well as more modern reading platforms – affect the ways in which literary works are understood: intermedial literary studies. Students will be introduced to non-traditional forms of (material) textuality that redefine what is commonly understood by a text. As such, the course will familiarise students with media that were inscribed with literary meaning by producers of objects – ranging from ceramics, furniture, and jewellery, among others – which in different ways conditioned the reception of literature. It will outline modern approaches in reception and historical media studies, including adaptation studies, as well as foster an understanding of three best-selling eighteenth-century works and one twentieth-century work. By the time students complete the course, they will have learnt about the complexities of mediation, reception, and adaptation, as well as how non-traditional textual forms introduced knowledge of literary works to those who had not necessarily read them.

教学目标

This course aims to introduce students to a media-centred history of literature that argues in favour of texts as unstable and adaptable, rather than as static. Literary works were changeable and polyphonous and existed in numerous versions and iterations, ranging from abridgments and redactions to film versions and objects inscribed with their meaning. The course will provide detailed studies of how literary works existed and knowledge of them circulated in different media and how these remediations in non-book form affected understanding of these works in ways that remain to be charted. Focusing on providing an alternative account of textual production and transmission, as well as reception, the course will offer a novel way of apprehending literature.

教学方式

Lectures, discussion, in-class presentations by students, written assignments

教学内容与进度安排

教学内容及预期效果 Content & Expected Achievement Introduction Alternative Notions of Text and Literature and the Importance of Medium and Mediation: Intermediality Studies Explained This session will introduce students to new approaches to text that are concerned with a broader definition of the concept of the “text” and that take into account Jerome McGann’s notion of the “textual condition.” It will also introduce the text-technological framework developed by Elaine Treharne and Claude William. Finally, it will facilitate a practical exercise in intermedial criticism by providing definitions relating to the medium and mediality that are then applied to examples to be provided in class. Learning Outcomes: Students will have learnt how to conceive of “text” more broadly and more precisely, including the distinction between “text” and “work” that is frequently obscured by traditional literary criticism. They will have become familiar with the basics of medial criticism while being able to understand the generation of medial meaning as part of text-technological processes. Alternative Textualities This session will broaden students’ understanding of textual production by considering a wider range of textual forms (as well as forms and modes of transmission) than those on which traditional and supposedly standard literary-historiographical accounts are based. The concept of “remediation” will be explained as one inherent in the transmission of literary works. Not only will remediations in different materials be considered, but students will also be introduced to the transformative centrality of adaptation. The session’s focus on “adaptation” will be based on Linda Hutcheon’s definition of “adaptation” but not exclusively be understood in relation to film. Learning outcomes: Students will have acquired an understanding of two analytical concepts that will be revisited throughout the course. They will gain a more profound understanding of the instability and dynamism of textual production and develop a greater awareness of how literary works are encountered and experienced by readers beyond the literary edition. Intermediality, Media Events, Epitexts, and Tie-Ins Building on the basics of medial meaning developed in session 1, this session will provide a more sophisticated toolbox of concepts to facilitate the study of intermediality and of text-image constructs, in particular. The concept of the “media event” will be defined and exemplified in relation to Tolkien’s Lord of the Rings cycle. Gerard Genette’s notion of the “epitext” will help to understand in what ways the textual condition of Tolkien’s work subsequently contributed to generating textual constructs that were physically distinct from the printed books. In addition to the “epitext,” the concept of the “tie-in” will be introduced as a particular commercial aspect of modern franchises (which, however, had their origin in the eighteenth century). Learning Outcomes: Students will be able to understand relationships between literary works and their spin-offs in medial terms that facilitate knowledge and an understanding of literary works in the wider world – and independently of the physical book. The epitext will be introduced as a dominant concept for the study of literature through the media of textual objects. Intermediality will be shown to be a concept and practice common to the publication and dissemination of literature, even though traditional literary studies have not generally acknowledged the medial meaning-making involved in the apprehension of literature. The Pamela Media Event I: Textual-Visual Criticism This session will introduce students to the visual and material cultures that were produced in response to the popularity of Samuel Richardson’s Pamela in the 1740s. It will focus in particular on two sets of illustrations (the first set of illustrations for a pirated edition of Richardson’s novel and the authorized illustrations the publisher commissioned Francis Hayman and F.H. Gravelot to produce for his 1742 edition) and examine in what ways these illustrations promoted different readings and understandings of the work. Learning Outcomes: Students will be able to situate the different visual media as motivated not only by considerations of market demand but also by the desire on the part of the publishers to advance visual castings of Richardson’s heroine. The different illustrations offered contextualisations that emphasized the character’s ambiguity and the ways in which Richardson, in response to the earlier illustrations, sought to mute any such ambivalence in the way his female protagonist could be read. Beyond conceptual insight, students will learn how to “read” and decode graphic images and understand them in relation to (and as intermedial instances of) the work illustrated. The Pamela Media Event 2: Object Studies This session will offer detailed studies of the objects that were produced as part of the Pamela vogue. A special focus will be placed on how the paintings by Hayman and Gravelot functioned in particular social settings in which they were displayed and beheld. These objects will be contextualized both in terms of their object properties (including their associated meanings) and their visual inscription (specifically the ways in which the illustrations convey literary meaning). Students will also be familiarized with Joseph Highmore’s series of Pamela paintings and their reproductions as furniture prints. Learning Outcomes: By focusing on both the material and associated (symbolic) properties of Pamela-related objects, on the one hand, and on these objects’ literary-visual inscription, on the other, students will be able to consolidate their understanding of how medial transfer and the reuse of iconic information operate. They will comprehend reading processes to be contingent on specific historical and medial factors and, as a consequence, will be able to interrogate assumptions regarding the readability of texts that have been formulated on the basis of printed instances of texts. The Seasons and the Commodification of a Poem: Making Present a Text While the case studies of Pamela-related objects focused on an early instance of a media event, this session will provide a broad contextualization of a media event related to a work that was fundamentally driven by the legal ramifications of the end of perpetual copyright. The material responses to Thomson’s poem were such that a much greater number of objects were produced the makers of which utilized the latest reproduction techniques to capitalize on the reputation of Thomson’s work. Visualizations in planar and plastic form captured characters from the poet’s interpolated episodes and the number of illustrated editions increased significantly in the late 1770s, turning – by the end of the century – The Seasons into the most frequently illustrated work. This session will primarily focus on the illustrated editions of the poem that were published before 1800. As part of a practical exercise, students will discuss different sets of illustrations and the ways in which these printed images generated an iconic identity for the poem. The illustrations introduced to students will form the basis of the transmedial analysis of visually enhanced epitexts to be studied in session 7. Learning Outcomes: Students will gain an understanding of the legal processes affecting the publishing of a literary classic, including the important marketing role that book illustrations possessed for demarcating one publisher’s edition from another’s. Comparing different sets of illustrations, they will be able to determine in what ways different artists proposed different interpretations of The Seasons. The Object Cultures of The Seasons (I) Building on the ground-work of session 6, this session will introduce the large number of objects – ranging from ceramics, fabrics, paintings, and furniture to sculpture – that advanced distinctly different interpretive renderings of characters and scenes from Thomson’s work. Highlighting the multifarious use of images (which had been generated in print culture) on non-paper-based objects, this session concerns itself with transmedia uses of illustrations and the readability of illustrated objects. Learning outcomes: Students will become familiar with processes of transmedia and relational meaning-making procedures. They will be able to trace how the same images function differently and advance different interpretations, depending on the object and material to which they are transferred. The Object Cultures of The Seasons (II) While the objects hitherto introduced were produced as part of the first wave of the media event of the 1770s, the objects and remediations featured in this class were largely produced in the nineteenth century. In addition to the extensive diversification of subjects illustrated at the time, The Seasons was appropriated to ever new contexts, in the process being re-interpreted numerous times. In addition to furnishing students with an account of the diversifying object culture of the period, the session will introduce the Etching Club’s 1842 edition of the poem. It will study how the numerous illustrations commissioned for the edition reflected new multi-artist practices of visual interpretation and how a contest between typographical text and extensive illustrative apparatus could occur within such an edition that had previously been prevented by the primacy of the printed word. Learning outcomes: Apart from a better understanding of the scope and diversity of the nineteenth-century object cultures deriving from The Seasons, students will learn, through a detailed analysis of the illustrations to the Etching Club edition, how illustrations functioned as part of the multi-medial make-up of the book. They will also be able to consider issues of multiple authorship and questions of authorial intentionality when relating to illustrated works, as well as how, by the mid-nineteenth century, the signifying role of illustrations was recognized by critics. What is a Classic? Reconsidering the Medial Lives of Robinson Crusoe Centrally concerned with the definition and contextualization of one of the earliest “modern” classics, this session will discuss the significance of visualizations in the dissemination of knowledge of the work. Focusing on three illustrated editions of the novel, the session will chart the different interpretive foci that illustrators developed in the early 1720s. It will then proceed to examinations of illustrations produced in the 1790s and probe how they were adapted for transmedial use on dining plates in both France and Britain. Learning outcomes: Continuing engagement with illustrations as gateways to literary meaning will hone students’ interpretive-analytical skills in identifying differences between different sets of illustrations and how these differences affected the reading experience. Likewise, the transmediation of designs on plates will be embedded within relative contexts of immersive, domestic reading experience and contribute to underpinning a notion of an intermedially constructed social text. Robinson Crusoe Reinvented: Competing Interpretations (I) Moving away from the illustrated edition form to objects ranging from playing cards to multifarious merchandise, this session concerns itself with the role Robinson Crusoe has played as part of consumer and entertainment culture more generally. It will pay attention to performative uses of the work and how it was harnessed to promote particular goods such as “colonial” wares (such as coffee and cocoa), in the process contextualizing the novel as part of a fiction of empire that is frequently supported by how the characters are cast visually. Learning outcomes: Students will be able to recover the ways in which Robinson Crusoe was appropriated ideologically and especially as part of a consumer industry underpinned, in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, through colonial expansion. 15-minute student presentations 15-minute student presentations Robinson Crusoe Reinvented: Competing Interpretations (II) This session will introduce three film adaptations and focus on three iconic scenes and the ways in which each version reinvents and makes sense of Defoe’s novel. Special attention will be paid to the racial identity of Man Friday as well as the portrayal of Crusoe as lord of the island. Learning outcomes: Students will be able to embed the different filmic versions of Robinson Crusoe in contexts that were current at the time these productions were made and which rewrote the work so as to cohere with dominant ideologies regarding empire and race. They will, furthermore, be able to reflect on the medial difference of film and those other object forms studied in the course. The Transmediation of Laurence Sterne’s Poor Maria Building on earlier research on the silkwork pictures of Charlotte at the Tomb of Werter, this session will introduce the multifarious object culture that developed around Sterne’s sentimental character, Poor Maria. It will focus on the transmedial use of Angelica Kauffman’s design of Maria and discuss the uses and appropriations of the design on objects, ranging from silkwork pictures to jewellery. Learning outcomes: Students will learn about relational meaning-making and especially the ways in which objects’ semiotic modality can affect the meanings of a literary illustration (and, extension, the interpretation of the literary work visualized). Media and Reception History This session will assemble various examples from previous classes to demonstrate the centrality of media-centred approaches to modern reception studies. It will revisit questions of how to write accounts of reception that involve media and how to reflect historiographically the dynamic and mobile character of literature. This session aims to cater to specific questions that students may have encountered while preparing their end-of-semester assignment and will encourage the sharing of research experience among students. Learning outcomes: Students will self-consciously reflect on their use of media-centred approaches and what questions they confronted as they revised some of the assumptions that had underpinned their understanding of literature before they took part in the course. Towards a “New” History of English Literature This concluding session will consider in what ways the study of the materials, contexts, and practices affecting literature will contribute to the writing of a new kind of English literary history. It will pay special attention to how reading practices have always been intermedial and to what this recovery of historical reading habits facilitates in terms of understanding how pervasive literature was in the past and continues to be in the present. Learning outcomes: Students are expected to have developed a much more nuanced understanding of how literature is perceived and apprehended inter- and multi-medially by readers. They will have gained a better sense of the multifarious mechanisms of mediation. Also, they will have realized that different versions of a work circulated simultaneously at any given time, thereby making possible a more pluralistic notion of literary production than has been offered by previous critical frameworks.
