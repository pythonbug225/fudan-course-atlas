课程简介（中文）

本课程将纵览十八世纪儿童文学萌芽期至首个“黄金时代”（约1915年）结束期间，专为儿童创作并由儿童消费的文学作品。课程重点探讨儿童的历史性阅读实践，并剖析插图、节选本、改编作品及期刊等多元媒介形式的发展对儿童文学的影响。 课程开篇将探讨历史上对儿童的不同认知模式：作为个体、作为消费者、作为受教育对象。这些儿童观将在课程中反复出现并深入展开（包括洛克主义、浪漫主义及维多利亚时期的儿童教育观）。课程前半部分引导学生接触各类儿童文学与印刷文化形式，最终聚焦首部儿童小说的诞生。后半部分则通过罗伯特·巴兰坦、弗朗西斯·霍奇森·伯内特、查尔斯·金斯利等作家的经典作品，梳理儿童小说子类型的重大发展脉络。

课程简介（英文）

This course will survey literature produced for and consumed by children from the beginnings of children’s literature in the eighteenth century to the end of the first “Golden Age” of children’s literature (c.1915). It foregrounds the historical reading practices of children and explores the impact of multimedia developments in the forms of illustration, abridgements, adaptations and periodicals. The course begins by exploring different historical ways the child has been conceived of in terms of a person, a consumer, and someone to be educated. These models of the child are returned to and further developed throughout the course (including Lockean, Romantic and Victorian perspectives in child education). The first half of the course introduces students to a range of children’s literary and print-culture forms, ending with the first novel written for children. The second half of the course charts some of the major developments in the sub-genres of children’s fiction through classic works by Robert M. Ballantyne, Frances Hodgson Burnett, and Charles Kingsley.

教学目标

The course aims to introduce students to the history children’s literature, covering works by some of the most important writers and publishers. The course seeks to ground students in current directions of research in the study of historical children’s literature by introducing students to the importance of print culture studies and the role of historical reading practices to understanding how children read and interacted with literary works historically. Students will learn how character development becomes central to children’s fiction, and the multiple and varied ways in which realism is employed in a range of sub-genres. Students will be encouraged to consult historical editions of the texts made available digitally and through Fudan University Library Special Collections. A key feature of the course is the development of a curated digital exhibition. The task engages students directly with children’s print culture (illustration and book design) and themes central to the study of children’s literature, including text-based literary analysis, knowledge of specific socio-historical contexts, and multimedia practices of reading. The task develops key literary and transferable skills relevant to future employment in the arts and heritage sector, such as research design, digital literacy, text work (close reading) ability, visual analysis, and curatorial skills.

教学方式

The course blends lectures with student-focused seminars. The seminar sections of classes revolve round textual analysis, engagement with primary sources, and multiple approaches to interpretating children’s texts. Each class offers opportunity for speaking and for developing critical thinking skills by crafting responses to the excerpts of texts examined in class. Core reading of primary and secondary literature is supported by mobile digital technology in the form of multiple-choice-answer quizzes. The assessment methods for this course are designed to engage students with a problem-oriented task that develops new skills while also requiring the creative application of existing skills.

教学内容与进度安排

Week 1. What is Children’s Literature? This first class introduces students to: the aim of the course, including assessment; the study of children’s literature and digital resources; bibliography and multimedia approaches to children’s literature. Learning outcomes: Students will become familiar with the contents of the course, the means of assessment, and with the central challenges of studying children’s literature. They will start to understand the centrality of design, print technologies, and illustration to children’s books. Week 2. Historicizing the Child This session will survey the multiple ways in which the child was conceived of between 1700 and 1900: the sinful child, the romantic child, the working child, and the sacred child. It will also introduce ideas regarding the education of children from John Locke, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, and Maria Edgeworth. Learning outcomes: Students will become familiar with the principal ways in which children have been characterized and conceived of by adults and educationalists. The readings in class enable students to contrast these changing ideas. Week 3. John Newbery and the Beginnings of Children’s Literature John Newbery’s name is synonymous with the rise of children’s literature. This session will focus on the development of the children’s books issued by the Newbery family of publishers. It will examine Newbery’s first children’s book, A Little Pretty Pocket Book, and the first periodical produced for children, The Lilliputian Magazine. Learning outcomes: Many of Newbery’s children’s books were generically hybrid, incorporating tales, rhymes, histories, morals, and games, as well as illustrations. Students will be able to identify the relevant parts of the publications, while also being able to differentiate between educational and entertainment elements. Week 4. Animal Fables Many animal fables have very long histories and have been reworked and retold throughout the ages for different purposes and not always for children. This session will survey animal fables, examining Aesop as the source for some from Newbery’s Pretty Little Pocket Book, Maria Edgeworth’s “Trusty Dog,” and Rudyard Kipling’s The Jungle Book (1894). Learning outcomes: Students will be able to trace the lineage of past and more recent animal fables, especially those originating in Aesop’s Fables. They will be able to explain what a fable is, its purpose, and why animals so frequently appear in them. Week 5. Fairy Tales Many fairy tales that are traditionally thought of as British in fact originate in the French tales of Charles Perrault and the German stories of the Brothers Grimm. This session explores how these tales became both “British” and a regular part of childhood reading. It will follow the tale of “Little Red Riding Hood” through multiple reiterations from the last two centuries. Learning outcomes: Students will begin to appreciate the transnational nature of much literature and learn to question what is the “original” or “authentic” version of a text. Students will also see how fairy tales are frequently adapted and re-written to suit the social values of the times. Week 6. Shakespeare for Children Charles and Mary Lamb’s Tales from Shakespeare (1807) concretized the practice of children reading Shakespeare in redacted from and was popular for well over 150 years. This session examines the influence of English literature’s most famous author on works for children. The processes and choices of adaptation will be examined through the example of Hamlet. Learning outcomes: Students will learn that major canonical works like Shakespeare’s plays were thought so nationally important that they were re-written for children as short prose narratives. Students will also see how the process of re-writing was undertaken in the early nineteenth century. Week 7. The First Children Novel: The Governess (1749) Sarah Fielding’s The Governess (1749) is generally considered the first English novel written for children. This session builds on the shorter fiction presented in previous sessions, using them to explain and contrast with the narrative techniques used by Fielding in her longer work for children. The class will demonstrate how Lockean ideals were employed to make literature for children both educational and entertaining. Learning outcomes: Students will become familiar with this landmark text and its later incarnations, understating how educational philosophy impacted on the novel and its later adaptations. Week 8. Project Workshop Students will pitch their ideas for their digital curation projects. Learning outcome: Students will receive feedback and guidance on their project ideas, enabling them to develop and improve their written proposals. Week 9. Adventure Fiction This session explores the origins of adventure fiction for children, taking the robinsonade as its paradigm. It will chart the development of the genre from early adaptations of Defoe’s Robinson Crusoe for children through to The Coral Island: A Tale of the Pacific Ocean (1858). Learning outcomes: Students will be able to identify the main defining elements of a robinsonade, and understand The Coral Island and the generic differences between it and Defoe’s work. Week 10. Imperialism in Children’s Literature This session focus on the social hierarchies and racial distinctions Ballantyne makes, connecting them with early Victorian imperial discourse. Learning outcomes: Students will be able to identify elements of imperialism in The Coral Island and relate them to an imperial and patriotic pedagogy. Students will be able to recognize how and articulate why imperial ideology frequently occurs in nineteenth-century children’s fiction. Week 11. Politics and Science in Children’s Literature This session starts an exploration of fantasy literature as it transpired in the mid-Victorian era. Charles Kingsley was influenced enormously by political events and scientific developments of his day and the class will investigate how in The Water-Babies: A Fairy Tale for a Land Baby (1863) he weaves together a commentary on Darwinism and the American Civil War. Learning outcomes: Students will be able to appreciate that children’s literature frequently reflects the social concerns of the day and understand the impact of scientific and international political developments on children’s literature, as well as its appeal to adults. Week 12. Education Policy and Social Inequality Kingsley’s The Water-Babies operates on many levels. Students will learn how the novel provides a robust commentary on social inequality and satirizes newly-developed education policy, both of which Kingsley achieves by through a direct narrative style which combines aspects of realism and fairy-tale. Learning outcomes: Students will be able to identify both realist and fairy-tale aspects in Kingsley’s writing, and be able to explain how they operate in conjunction with each other to create social critique that is both poignant and entertaining. Week 13: The Female Explorer This session will introduce the setting of Frances Hodgson Burnett’s The Secret Garden (1911), explaining how the moorland serves as a metaphor for Mary’s changed circumstances, before explaining Burnett’s use of language in the characterization of Martha and Mrs. Medlock. Finally, Mary is cast as an explorer within the domestic realm, allowing her curiosity to overcome the loss of her parents and contrary personality. Learning outcomes: Focusing on early chapters, students will learn how Burnett constructs Mary as an anti-romantic child, and how she is deracinated from everything she knows, as a victim of empire, in addition to Burnett’s methods of characterization and creating setting. Week 14: Nature and Healing This session will introduce the ways in which Burnett defines nature as both healing and instructive, focusing on the characters of Mary, Colin and Dickon and their understanding of “magic.” Tending the garden and spending time in it heals Mary psychologically, while Colin is physically rejuvenated. Learning outcomes: Students will be able to identify the multiple ways in which Burnet incorporates nature into the narrative and the positive transformations attached to it. Week 15. Exhibition Workshop Students will present their finished digital curation projects to the class, receiving questions and feedback. Learning outcomes: Students will practice their presentation skills, applying them from to presenting their digital curation projects rather than a traditional academic presentation. The digital curation project fosters transferable skills including research design, selecting appropriate sources, analyzing literary texts and paratexts, and digital visualization. Week 16: Course Review In this final session, the main points of the course will be drawn together. Time will be given to questions and extended discussion on central topics covered in the course. Learning outcome: Students will consolidate their understanding of children’s literature in terms of genre and print culture.
